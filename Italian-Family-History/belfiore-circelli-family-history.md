# From San Bartolomeo in Galdo to New Rochelle: The Belfiore and Circelli Family History

The Belfiore-Circelli family story traces a clear arc from a hilltop farming town in Campania's Fortore Valley to the working-class Italian neighborhoods of New Rochelle, New York — part of a massive chain migration that transplanted entire communities from the Province of Benevento to the northeastern United States between 1890 and 1920. This report synthesizes findings across immigration records, death indexes, cemetery databases, Italian civil records, ship manifests, historical archives, and genealogical databases. **Several key records were confirmed, important new hypotheses established, and critical dead ends documented** to guide the next phase of research.

---

## The patriarch and matriarch: Leonardantonio Belfiore and Maria Donata Circelli

### A foundling's name: the origin of "Belfiore"

A translated marriage record from the Comune di San Bartolomeo in Galdo confirms the marriage of **Leonardantonio Belfiore** to **Maria Donata Circelli** on **January 27, 1884**. This document transforms several key assumptions about the family.

First, the patriarch's name was not "Anthony" or "Antonio" — it was **Leonardantonio**, a compound given name repeated multiple times throughout the marriage record. American records later shortened this to "Anthony" or "Antonio," a common pattern of Americanization.

Second, and most significantly, Leonardantonio was a **foundling** — the son of *padre ignoto* AND *madre ignota* (both parents unknown). This means **"Belfiore" was not an inherited family surname at all.** It was assigned to him, almost certainly by a foundling home — the *Ruota dei Proietti*, the revolving door where unwanted infants were anonymously deposited. Italian authorities gave these children invented surnames, often beautiful or aspirational ones. "Belfiore" — *bel fiore*, beautiful flower — fits this naming pattern precisely. **The Belfiore line cannot be traced further back than Leonardantonio himself.** Any connection to the noble Conti Belfiore of Umbria/Sicily is definitively ruled out; the surname was a civic creation, not an inheritance.

Third, Maria Donata's parents are now identified from the marriage record: her father was **Giuseppe Circelli** and her mother was **Marianna Mita**, both already deceased by 1884. This anchors the Circelli family line and connects Maria Donata directly to the broader Circelli clan of San Bartolomeo in Galdo — Salvatore and Michele Circelli, who arrived together on the Alsatia in 1898, were almost certainly her brothers or nephews.

The ages in the marriage record help anchor the timeline. Leonardantonio was 23 in January 1884, placing his birth around **1860–1861**. Maria Donata was 27, putting her birth around **1856–1857**. Both were illiterate — they could not sign their own names. One generation later, their son Michael signed his 1918 draft card in a clear hand. In a single generation, the family went from illiterate farmers to a literate tradesman in New Rochelle.

### Did Leonardantonio come to America?

The evidence strongly suggests **no**. Maria traveled under her maiden name — the 1896 ship record lists her as "Circelli, Maria Doreta," not Belfiore. After 12 years of marriage, a woman traveling with her husband would typically appear under his surname. Her use of "Circelli" suggests she was traveling without him, likely within her Circelli family network.

No immigration record for Leonardantonio Belfiore (or any variant spelling) has been found. Michael's draft cards don't mention a father — the 1918 card lists his nearest relative as "(Child) Dorothy Belfiore," and the 1942 card lists "Wife, same address." Neither references a living father.

The most likely explanation is that **Leonardantonio died in Italy before 1896**, which would explain why Maria reverted to her maiden name for the Atlantic crossing and why her Circelli brothers followed her to America shortly after — possibly to help support a widowed sister and her young son Michael. Alternatively, he could have preceded her under a different or shortened name, or simply stayed behind. His death certificate from San Bartolomeo in Galdo, if it exists in the civil records, would resolve this question.

### Leonardantonio's death index entry

**A death is confirmed** in the New York State Death Index for an Anthony Belfiore who died **July 11, 1938**, in New Rochelle, NY, certificate number **42184**. A second Anthony Belfiore died September 15, 1938, in Beacon, NY (certificate #51555) — a different individual. The July 11 date and New Rochelle location match the known family record. However, given the marriage record's revelation that the patriarch's name was Leonardantonio (not Anthony), the death certificate may list him under any variant — "Leonardantonio," "Leonard Antonio," "Leonard A.," or simply "Anthony." Ordering this certificate ($22 from the NY State Department of Health) remains a high-priority action; because he was a foundling, the "parents' names" fields will likely read "unknown" — but the certificate will still reveal his age at death, cause of death, burial location, and the informant's name.

**Important caveat:** If Leonardantonio died in Italy before Maria's 1896 departure, then the Anthony Belfiore who died in New Rochelle in 1938 may be a *different* Anthony Belfiore — possibly the Frank Belfiore discovery discussed below, or another relative. The 1900 census for New Rochelle is the critical record: if it shows an Anthony/Leonardantonio Belfiore in the household, he did make it to America. If it shows only Maria and young Michael, he almost certainly died in Italy.

No obituary was found online; the New Rochelle *Standard Star* from mid-July 1938, available on microfilm at the New Rochelle Public Library, is the most promising source.

**Maria Doreta Circelli's arrival on the S.S. Oregon on December 2, 1896** is consistent with the known details. The specific manifest should be searchable through Stephen P. Morse's One-Step tool at stevemorse.org or via Ancestry.com's New York Passenger Lists, searching "Circelli" arriving December 1896. No death record for Maria was found in online databases. She likely died before the Social Security Death Master File era (1960s onward), meaning her death would appear in the NY State Death Index under either "Maria Belfiore" (married name) or "Mary Belfiore." The death certificate, once located, should confirm her parents as Giuseppe Circelli and Marianna Mita — corroborating the marriage record.

**A potentially significant discovery**: A **Frank Belfiore** appears in records as born November 1897 in New Rochelle, with parents listed as **Antonio Belfiore and Maria D. Circelli**. These parents match Leonardantonio and Maria exactly (with the expected Americanization of the name), suggesting Frank was a previously unidentified **sibling of Michael Kirtus Belfiore**. Frank died April 21, 1968, in Daytona Beach, Florida. But note — if Leonardantonio died in Italy before 1896, then "Antonio Belfiore" on Frank's records may be a posthumous attribution, or the father's name was simply carried forward from the family's knowledge. This finding merits immediate follow-up through census records and Florida death records.

## Michael Kirtus Belfiore: the central figure

Michael Kirtus Belfiore's death on January 16, 1952, in New Rochelle is confirmed. His burial at **Holy Sepulchre Cemetery** (95 Kings Highway, New Rochelle) is established, though no Find A Grave memorial exists — the cemetery's online coverage is significantly incomplete. The 1952 New York State Death Index is available through the Internet Archive (released by Reclaim the Records), where his entry should appear under Belfiore with a certificate number for ordering the full death certificate.

The name "Kirtus" is unusual and likely an Americanization or phonetic rendering of an Italian middle name. His Italian birth name was almost certainly **Michele Belfiore**, and Italian civil records from San Bartolomeo in Galdo (FamilySearch catalog #2614757, covering 1862–1942) should contain his birth registration under that name. Born December 15, 1883 or 1885, he would have been a child or teenager when the family immigrated. Given the marriage date of January 27, 1884, Maria Donata may have already been pregnant at the wedding if Michael was born in December 1883 — or he arrived shortly after if born in 1885.

His **1918 WWI draft registration** places him at 114 Murray Avenue, New Rochelle, employed by "Witmark" at 8 Dante Street, New Rochelle. His nearest relative was listed as "(Child) Dorothy Belfiore" at 216 Hamilton Street, Bridgeport, Fairfield County, Connecticut. By 1942, his WWII registration shows him at 48 Coligni Avenue, New Rochelle, with employer "Alfred A. Last" at 141 Chauncey Avenue, New Rochelle. Both employers are discussed in the historical context section below. His occupation as a **house painter** was entirely typical for Italian immigrants — painting, masonry, stucco work, and building trades were core occupations in the Italian-American community.

His citizenship status on the WWI card is notable: he claimed citizenship through **"Father's Papers"** — meaning his father's naturalization before Michael reached majority. This is significant because it implies that either Leonardantonio *did* come to America and was naturalized, or that someone else (perhaps a stepfather or the father's naturalization was claimed in error) held those papers. This is a thread worth pulling.

No obituary, naturalization record, or immigration record for Michael was found through free online sources. The New Rochelle *Standard Star* from January 17–20, 1952, should contain his obituary. Naturalization records may be held at the Westchester County Clerk's Office (records from 1908–1936), the Connecticut State Library (if naturalized in Bridgeport), or NARA's New York facility.

## The mystery of Dorothy Belfiore

Dorothy Belfiore appears as "Child" on Michael's 1918 WWI registration card at 216 Hamilton Street, Bridgeport, Connecticut — and then vanishes from the historical record. She does not appear in the 1940 census household, which lists only sons Edward and Joseph. **No birth, death, census, or cemetery record for Dorothy Belfiore was found in Connecticut or elsewhere.**

The most compelling hypothesis is **death during the 1918 influenza pandemic**. The timing is precise: Michael's draft registration was filed in September 1918 — the exact month the Spanish Flu arrived in Connecticut (first case September 11 in New London). Bridgeport was devastated, recording 69 deaths within the first five weeks alone, with the epidemic continuing into 1919. Connecticut lost approximately 9,000 residents — nearly 1% of its population. Children, while not the flu's primary demographic target, still died in substantial numbers, and secondary infections like pneumonia were especially lethal for the young.

Three alternative scenarios warrant consideration. First, Dorothy may have died from other childhood diseases common in industrial-era Bridgeport — diphtheria, measles, or whooping cough. Second, she could have been a daughter from a **possible first marriage** before Frances Towey, which would explain both her presence in 1918 and absence later. Third, she could have been a niece or relative's child temporarily in Michael's household. The 1920 census for Bridgeport is the critical record: if Dorothy appears, she survived the flu; if she is absent, a pre-1920 death is nearly certain. Connecticut vital records (available through Ancestry.com and the Connecticut State Library) should contain a death record if she died in the state.

## Frances Towey Belfiore: a cross-cultural marriage

The surname **Towey is definitively Irish**, deriving from the Gaelic **Ó Tuathaigh**. The Towey sept traces to the chief of Bredagh, a small territory in County Mayo, with the family concentrated around Ballaghaderreen in County Roscommon across four parishes: Kilcolman, Castlemore, Kilbeagh, and Kilmovee.

The marriage of Frances Towey to Michael Belfiore was **notably counter-cultural for its era**. Irish-Italian marriages were rare before World War II. A 1920s study found that Irish New Yorkers were more likely to marry a German Jew than an Italian. The two communities clashed over jobs, housing, and parish control despite sharing Catholicism. Only 8% of Italian Americans born before 1920 had mixed ancestry. The major wave of Irish-Italian intermarriage came after WWII, driven by shared Catholic schools and suburbanization.

If Michael and Frances married circa 1920–1922 (before Edward's birth around 1923), their union preceded this wave by decades. **Shared Catholic faith was the primary bridge** between the communities, and the family's burial at Holy Sepulchre Cemetery (a Catholic cemetery) confirms their Catholic identity. No marriage record was found online; it may exist in Bridgeport or New Rochelle church records, or in Connecticut/New York State marriage indexes on Ancestry.com.

## The next generation: Edward, Joseph, and William

**Edward Belfiore** (born circa 1923) would have been draft-age during WWII. No military service records were found through free databases, but the 1973 fire at the National Personnel Records Center destroyed nearly 80% of Army records from 1912–1960. Crucially, Edward does not appear on the New York State WWII Honor List of Dead and Missing, indicating he likely survived the war if he served. No obituary or Find A Grave memorial was located.

**Joseph Belfiore** (born circa 1926–1928), father of Robert Belfiore, has a possible but unconfirmed match: a Joseph Belfiore who died June 30, 2016, in the Spring Hill, Florida, area (Brewer & Sons Funeral Home), born March 15, 1926. The birth year is close to the 1940 census estimate, and Florida retirement is common among New York-area families. However, the obituary contained no biographical details confirming parents or children. A separate Joseph J. Belfiore (born August 22, 1925, died October 21, 2010, Lindenhurst, NY) also appeared in the SSDI. The 1950 census (now publicly available) for New Rochelle should help disambiguate.

**William Belfiore** remains the most elusive. No records were found connecting a William Belfiore to the New Rochelle family. His relationship to Michael — whether son, brother, or other — could not be determined. City directories and the 1950 census are the best next steps.

## Unraveling the Circelli family connections

The Circelli surname is one of the **most prominent families in San Bartolomeo in Galdo**, with a street (Via Pasquale Circelli) named for the family. ItalyHeritage.com's indexed birth records from 1869–1882 reveal dozens of Circelli births, organized into distinct family groups.

Now that the marriage record identifies Maria Donata's parents as **Giuseppe Circelli and Marianna Mita**, the question becomes: how do Salvatore and Michele Circelli (the 1898 Alsatia passengers) connect to Giuseppe's branch? If Giuseppe had multiple children, Salvatore (b. 1869) and Michele (b. 1871) could be Maria Donata's younger brothers — the age gap is plausible given Maria Donata's birth circa 1856–1857. Alternatively, they could be nephews through another Circelli line. The ItalyHeritage.com birth records show a Salvatore born January 1, 1869, and a Michele born March 12, 1871, both sons of **Leonardo Circelli and Rosa Pacifico** — a *different* parent set from Giuseppe Circelli and Marianna Mita. This means Salvatore and Michele were likely **cousins** of Maria Donata rather than siblings, connected through the broader Circelli clan.

Their consecutive placement on the ship manifest (lines 8 and 9) confirms they traveled together as family. The Leonardo Circelli household also produced children named Bartolomeo (b. 1873), Donato (b. 1875), and Pasquale (b. 1880).

Salvatore's reappearance on the **S.S. Sant'Anna** sailing from Naples November 14, 1911, strongly suggests a **return voyage**. This "birds of passage" pattern was extraordinarily common — an estimated 50% of Southern Italian immigrants returned at least once. At age 42, Salvatore may have been visiting family, settling affairs, or returning after a period back in Italy.

**Antonio Maria Circelli** (born February 20, 1862, parents Bartolomeo Circelli and Laura Pannone) represents yet another branch of the Circelli family tree. His father Bartolomeo Circelli may connect to the Leonardo Circelli branch through standard Italian naming patterns — Leonardo named a son Bartolomeo (b. 1873), likely after his own father. If so, Leonardo and Antonio Maria could be brothers sharing the same father, Bartolomeo. However, Antonio Maria's mother (Laura Pannone) differs from the Giuseppe Circelli/Marianna Mita line, confirming these are **separate Circelli branches** within the same town.

A remarkable Find A Grave discovery: **Govanni Cicelli (1858–1906)** is buried at Holy Sepulchre Cemetery in New Rochelle (Memorial ID 175634192). "Cicelli" is almost certainly a misspelling of "Circelli." Born 1858, he was a contemporary of both Antonio Maria Circelli (b. 1862) and Maria Donata Circelli (b. ~1856–1857) and could be a sibling or close relative from the Giuseppe Circelli line. His 1906 death places him among the earliest wave of San Bartolomeo immigrants in New Rochelle.

The **2023 obituary of Natalina Circelli née Apicella** provides direct modern confirmation of the migration chain: born December 25, 1935, in **San Bartolomeo in Galdo**, she died March 17, 2023, in **New Rochelle**. Her husband Giuseppe Circelli (d. 1998) was also from New Rochelle. She emigrated in October 1960, initially to Union Township, NJ, then moved to New Rochelle in 1962.

## San Bartolomeo in Galdo: a town that bled its people

San Bartolomeo in Galdo sits at **597 meters elevation** on a hilltop overlooking the Fortore Valley, the northeasternmost municipality in Campania, bordering both Molise and Puglia. Its name combines the patron Saint Bartholomew with "Galdo" from the medieval Latin *gualdus* (forest) or German *Wald*. In the 1880s, the town held approximately **7,700 residents** sustained by subsistence agriculture — wheat, maize, olives, grapes, and historically significant silk production.

The forces driving emigration were devastating and compounding. The post-unification Italian government imposed crushing taxes on wheat and salt — necessities for southern peasant families. The global transport revolution of the 1870s–1880s flooded European markets with cheap American and Russian wheat and Chinese and Indian silk, destroying San Bartolomeo's two key cash products. The phylloxera epidemic of the 1880s devastated Benevento's renowned vineyards. Cholera epidemics struck repeatedly — most catastrophically in 1884. The Province of Benevento suffered major earthquakes in 1688, 1805, and later in 1930 and 1962, with San Bartolomeo itself gravely damaged in both 20th-century quakes.

The parish baptismal records show the emigration signal clearly: baptisms exceeded 300 annually until 1903, then dropped to 245 in 1904, 234 in 1906, and 219 in 1907 as males were leaving en masse. The population collapsed only after WWII, falling to 4,314 by 2023.

Leonardantonio's foundling status adds a poignant dimension to this story. As an orphan with an invented surname, he had no inherited land, no family network, no safety net — precisely the conditions that made emigration either irresistible or, if he died young, that left his widow and child with no choice but to rely on her Circelli relatives for the passage to America.

### The "S. Bartolomeo in Calor" name mystery solved

Ship manifests consistently record passengers' origin as "S. Bartolomeo in Calor." **"Calor" is the ancient Latin name for the Calore river** — the dominant geographic feature of Benevento province. San Bartolomeo in Galdo actually overlooks the Fortore river, not the Calore. The "in Calor" designation was almost certainly a regional geographic identifier applied by Neapolitan shipping clerks. No municipality called "San Bartolomeo in Calore" has ever existed. The combination of matching surnames, timeframe, and province conclusively identifies "S. Bartolomeo in Calor" as San Bartolomeo in Galdo.

## New Rochelle's Italian West End

Italian immigrants first arrived in New Rochelle in the **1880s as railroad laborers** laying track for what is now Metro North's New Haven Line. They settled in **West New Rochelle**, a densely built hillside neighborhood formerly called "Dutch Hill." By 1940, the WPA guide recorded approximately **5,000 descendants of early Italian laborers** in New Rochelle.

**St. Joseph's Church** (280 Washington Avenue), founded in **1901**, was the Italian ethnic parish. Originally a wooden chapel on 4th Street, it was rebuilt in red Harvard brick on Washington Avenue and consecrated by John Cardinal Farley in 1904. A parochial school opened in 1909. The church held an annual St. Anthony's Feast and Bazaar with a traditional Italian street procession. The **Calabria Mutual Aid Society** ("Casa Calabria"), founded 1936 at Union Avenue and Warren Street, served as a community hub.

Michael's addresses trace a life within this community: 114 Murray Avenue (1918) → 48 Coligni Avenue (1942) → 46 Mechanic Street (1952, death certificate). All are within the West End neighborhood.

### The employers on Michael's draft cards

**"Witmark" at 8 Dante Street, New Rochelle (1918)**: No definitive identification was found. This was not the famous M. Witmark & Sons music publishing house. "Witmark" was almost certainly an individual — likely a painting contractor or building trades employer. Dante Street, notably named for the Italian poet, signals an Italian residential neighborhood. New Rochelle city directories from 1915–1920 should contain a listing.

**"Alfred A. Last" at 141 Chauncey Avenue, New Rochelle (1942)**: Also unidentified online. The address is a single-family home in the Chauncey Estates neighborhood. This was most likely a private homeowner who employed Michael for residential painting work.

## Cemetery records: what exists and what remains hidden

**Holy Sepulchre Cemetery** (95 Kings Highway, New Rochelle) was founded in 1886 by Father McLoughlin of Blessed Sacrament Church. As the principal Catholic cemetery in New Rochelle, it served the Italian community.

**No Belfiore memorials exist on Find A Grave or BillionGraves** for this cemetery — a gap in online coverage rather than evidence of absence. The cemetery requires all genealogical inquiries in writing with a **$25 fee** (checks to Holy Sepulchre Cemetery, c/o Blessed Sacrament Church, 15 Shea Place, New Rochelle, NY 10801; email: blessedsacrametnr.hsc@gmail.com; phone: 914-636-6343).

The **Govanni Cicelli** burial (1858–1906, Find A Grave Memorial #175634192) at Holy Sepulchre is the only confirmed Circelli-variant burial found online.

## The Belfiore name: a foundling's beautiful flower

The marriage record definitively resolves the question of the Belfiore surname's origin. "Belfiore" derives from Italian *bel* (beautiful) + *fiore* (flower), and in this family's case, it was **assigned by a foundling institution to the infant Leonardantonio** — a child of unknown parents. This was standard practice in 19th-century Italy: foundling homes (*brefotrofi*) gave their charges invented surnames, often drawn from nature, virtues, or beauty. Names like Belfiore, Esposito ("exposed one"), Innocenti, Proietti ("projected," i.e., abandoned), and Diotallevi ("God raise him") are hallmarks of foundling origins.

The noble **Conti Belfiore** documented in Giovanni Battista di Crollalanza's 1888 *Dizionario Storico-Blasonico*, with a coat of arms featuring "azure with a golden garden lily," have **no connection whatsoever** to this family. Leonardantonio's "Belfiore" was a civic creation born of abandonment, not inheritance. While there is a certain poetry in the name — a beautiful flower given to a child with no roots — any claim to noble lineage is definitively ruled out by the marriage record.

This finding also means that **the Belfiore genealogical line terminates with Leonardantonio**. There are no Belfiore ancestors to trace further back. The family's deep roots run instead through Maria Donata's Circelli line — Giuseppe Circelli and Marianna Mita of San Bartolomeo in Galdo.

## Chain migration: a village transplanted across the Atlantic

The S.S. Alsatia manifest (List 128, arriving March 1898) carrying approximately **30 passengers from "S. Bartolomeo in Calor"** represents a textbook chain migration event — and the evidence strongly confirms that this migration chain reached New Rochelle and persists to this day.

Surnames from the Alsatia manifest and San Bartolomeo's traditional family list show confirmed New Rochelle presence for at least three families:

- **Circelli**: Govanni Cicelli buried at Holy Sepulchre Cemetery (1906); Michael "Mickey" Circelli (1930–2004) born in New Rochelle; Natalina Circelli (1935–2023) explicitly born in San Bartolomeo in Galdo, died in New Rochelle
- **Pepe**: The Pepe Auto Group operates luxury car dealerships across Westchester County; Pepe's Place Italian restaurant operates at 560 North Avenue, New Rochelle
- **Apicella**: Confirmed through Natalina Circelli née Apicella, born in San Bartolomeo in Galdo, and Antonio "Tony" Apicella (1931–2007), born in San Bartolomeo in Galdo to Nicholas and Maria Gracia (Circelli) Apicella

## Conclusion: what this research reveals and what remains hidden

This investigation confirms a richly documented chain migration from San Bartolomeo in Galdo to New Rochelle, with the Circelli family deeply embedded in both communities across more than a century. The marriage record is the most transformative single document in the collection: it reveals that the Belfiore surname was assigned to a foundling — Leonardantonio was a child of unknown parents — making the family name a beautiful invention rather than an inherited lineage. It identifies Maria Donata's parents as Giuseppe Circelli and Marianna Mita, anchoring the maternal line. And the evidence strongly suggests Leonardantonio never made it to America, meaning Maria Donata — a widow traveling under her maiden name, with a young son — relied on her Circelli relatives for the crossing and the new life that followed.

The discovery of Frank Belfiore (b. 1897, same parents) expands the known sibling group. Dorothy Belfiore's disappearance after the September 1918 draft registration, coinciding precisely with the Spanish Flu's arrival in Bridgeport, makes pandemic death the leading hypothesis. The Frances Towey marriage represents an unusually early Italian-Irish union in an era when such matches met significant cultural resistance from both communities. And Michael's claim of citizenship through "Father's Papers" raises a fascinating question about whether Leonardantonio was present in America after all — or whether this was a misunderstanding or creative claim on a draft card.

The most valuable single action is now **pulling the 1900 census** for any Belfiore household in New Rochelle. If Leonardantonio appears, the entire immigration narrative changes. If only Maria and Michael appear, the foundling-turned-father who gave this family its beautiful name likely rests in an unmarked grave in the hills above the Fortore Valley — and the story of this family in America begins with a young widow and her son, stepping off the S.S. Oregon into the December cold of New York Harbor, 1896.
